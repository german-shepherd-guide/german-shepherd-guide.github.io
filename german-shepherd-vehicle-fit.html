Which Cars and SUVs Do German Shepherd Owners Actually Drive?

Ask which cars and SUVs German Shepherd owners actually drive and you’ll hear about everything from a two-door Bronco with the rear seat folded to an Odyssey arranged around three crates. What makes their setups worth borrowing is how clearly they can tell you what had to move, how the crate went in, and who could still sit in the car afterward.

Quick answer. Owners describe hauling German Shepherds in a Bronco, Expedition, Odyssey, Quest and E350. If you’re shopping, test the doorway, usable floor and height from the ground with the actual seats and floor in position. A tall crate can clear the cargo ceiling and still catch on the hatch.

Which vehicles did German Shepherd owners report using?

Owners described using a Ford Bronco or Expedition for crated trips, a Honda Odyssey or Nissan Quest with seating space repurposed for crates, and a Ford E350 for trips that also called for sleeping space. The accounts show what worked in individual vehicles and give you specific questions to ask about yours.

In a Pedigree Database discussion about dog cars, one owner described room for crates and a recliner in an E350, plus previous use of Dodge Grand Caravans. Another reported moving from a Quest to an Odyssey, where removing a middle seat made space for a third crate. A hatchback owner reported that a crate with enough space for their German Shepherd’s ears wouldn’t fit in their Prius. That last case is a reported fit limit rather than an example of a working travel crate.

In a Bronco Nation discussion on crate travel, a German Shepherd handler reported hauling dogs in a two-door Bronco with the rear seat down and fitting two crates in an Expedition. These are first-person accounts. The model names identify the vehicles the owners described, and no account establishes fit for every year or trim.

What makes another owner’s report worth using?

Start with the exact seat position and crate model. A handler who can name the row they folded, the direction the door faces and the part of the hatch they had to clear gives you a setup you can reproduce. Ask about the trade-off that matters to your household. If you need the second row every weekend, a Bronco layout with the rear seat flat is useful evidence of what to test, and it also tells you what would have to change on your trips.

For two crates, sketch the footprint of both before you visit a lot. Write the location of each door and the side from which you expect to reach its latch. A second crate can occupy the same space you planned to stand in while opening the first. If a van owner describes removing a seat, also ask where the empty crate comes in and where they keep their gear once both crates are loaded.

What the 42- and 48-inch examples show

A crate that measures 42 inches along the outside leaves zero spare length against Subaru’s 42-inch seats-up maximum. A 48-inch crate exceeds that maximum by 6 inches. Both lengths are below the stated folded maximum. Take the height and width readings in the actual layout before you treat either as a candidate.

Consider a hypothetical crate 42 inches long, 28 inches wide and 30 inches high. Its 30-inch height is 2.1 inches below the published interior height and half an inch above the published opening height. That fails a straight-through height check. Bringing the empty crate to the car lets you see whether its shape allows a different entry path, and whether the hatch closes once the crate reaches its final position.

What changes with a sedan, crossover or minivan?

Compare the doorway and seating arrangement you will actually use. A sedan owner may use a side door, a crossover owner may rely on folding part of the second row, and a minivan owner may have both a side-door route and a rear hatch to measure.

If your current car already handles short outings, write down exactly what changes when you add luggage or passengers. Our German Shepherd walking routines illustrate how everyday outings differ between households. A quick trip to a familiar walk and a full weekend away place different demands on the same car.

The week you routinely live matters as much as your longest drive. If someone else takes your dog out while you work, use the same seating arrangement in both people's checks where possible. Our guide to planning around work shifts can help you identify which trips need the most convenient setup.

Ground-to-sill height and the step inside

Measure up from level ground to the top of the sill. Then measure any change from the sill to the cargo floor. A raised edge changes where you lift or tilt a rigid box, and the 2025 Outback figures show why the trim belongs on your notes. Subaru lists the Wilderness lift-over height 0.8 inch higher than the other figure in its specification sheet.

Which opening should you try first?

For a sedan, measure across the side doorway and check the space between the front seat and rear bench before assuming a rigid crate will turn into position. If you’re considering a crossover, start with the rear gate and look along the sides at the height of the crate lid. In a minivan, try the sliding door and rear gate separately. The side door might offer an easier first lift while the rear gate offers a straighter path to the final spot. Record which opening you actually intend to use at home and at your destination.

Picture the whole trip from parking space to arrival. Will the hatch open under your garage door? Will a wall leave enough room to angle the crate in, and will you be able to open the crate door with bags beside it? People who share loading duties will get more from a note about where to park or which door to open first than from a general claim that the cargo area is large.

How do you check a car before committing to it?

Bring the crate or a rigid mock-up and test the layout with the car parked. A careful measurement sheet makes it easier to compare a dealer's car, a rental, and the one already in your driveway.

  1. Set the seats for the trip. Put the front seats in the driving positions people actually use. Decide which rear seats must stay available and set the cargo floor or sliding deck where it will be used.
  2. Measure the outside of the crate. Include the widest handles, latches, feet or flanges that remain attached. Write the numbers down in length, width and height order.
  3. Measure the route in. Record the opening height and the clear width at the crate's top and bottom corners. Check the height from the ground as well as the internal lip.
  4. Measure the space after loading. Look at the narrowest wheel-arch gap, usable depth at the crate's top edge, and the lowest point over the intended footprint. Check whether the folded seat leaves a slope or step.
  5. Check the doors and remaining space. Close the hatch slowly while watching the clearance to the crate. Then open the crate door and confirm you can reach its latch. Set the actual bags or an equivalent mock-up alongside it.

If you have placed a cargo cover or liner for fur, measure with that layer installed. The German Shepherd shedding guide describes the owner routines behind that everyday cleanup. If a trip also includes a favorite toy, use the luggage check to see where it goes when the crate door opens. Compare two layouts before you change cars

Start with the car you have and record a seats-up layout and a layout with only the rear seats you can spare folded. Test them with the same crate dimensions. If the length check passes only with both seats down, you now know which passenger or luggage arrangement to test in a different car. If only the hatch height fails, look at the doorway of the next vehicle before you spend time measuring its entire cargo bay.

Ask anyone who helps load the crate to try the parked-car setup too. The path can feel different from each side of the hatch, especially when a garage wall limits where you can stand. Mark where you need room to turn and carry the empty crate before planning a layout around that opening.

Which mistakes invalidate a fit check?

Most come from comparing numbers measured in different places or different seat configurations. Keep a short label beside every measurement so you can reproduce it.

  • Using the vehicle's ground clearance. It describes space beneath the car. Your tape for this task goes up to the sill at the doorway you use.
  • Checking the floor and overlooking the hatch. A floor measurement does not describe what happens when the crate reaches a tapered corner.
  • Relying on the package name. Treat the printed size as a starting label and verify the actual usable exterior dimensions.
  • Measuring with the front seat unusually far forward. Set the car for the people who will drive it before comparing.
  • Reading the checker as a guarantee. Its result covers a straight dimensional comparison. Bring the actual crate to check the opening path and hatch closure.

Also distinguish fitting the setup from getting the dog used to it. If your young shepherd needs help building confidence with a new routine, the monthly training plan for teenage German Shepherds explains how to break practice into manageable steps. Finish the geometry check first so that practice happens in the setup you intend to keep.

Frequently asked questions

Which owners described room for multiple crates?

One owner described two crates in an Expedition. Another described making room for a third in an Odyssey by removing a middle seat.

Does a 42-inch crate fit with the back seats up?

The 2025 Outback lists 42 inches of maximum cargo length with its rear seatbacks up, leaving zero nominal length clearance for a 42-inch crate. Check the actual hatch and space at the crate’s upper corners.

Will folding the seats make a 48-inch crate fit?

Folding the seats can provide more length. Check the hatch opening, cargo shape, floor transitions and closed-hatch clearance with the actual crate.

What does loading height mean here?

It is the vertical distance from level ground to the top of the cargo sill for the measured rear-hatch examples. Measure the side entry separately if you use a side door.

Can I use the 2025 Outback figures for another model year?

Use the table to learn which dimensions matter. Take fresh readings on the specific newer vehicle with its own seating and floor arrangement.

Is cubic footage enough for comparing two cars?

It describes cargo volume. A rigid crate also needs a clear entry path, sufficient room at its corners, and clearance with the hatch closed.

Bring the crate measurements with you

Take the owner picks as leads and compare the actual cars by the doorway, usable floor and loading height. Write those numbers down with the model year and seating arrangement. The strongest recommendation you can pass to another owner is a fit you can describe clearly enough for them to repeat.
