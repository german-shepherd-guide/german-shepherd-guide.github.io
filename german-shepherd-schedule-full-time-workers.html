German Shepherd Daily Schedule for Full-Time Workers: Sample 9-to-5 and Long-Shift Routines That Cover Exercise, Training and Alone Time

Calm, realistic day plans for 9-to-5 jobs, puppies and long shifts, plus a planner that builds a schedule from your own hours.

Home›Blog›Schedule for full-time workers

Updated: October 18, 2026 · German Shepherd Guide · Built from shelter guidance, trainer methods, and real owner schedules

A German Shepherd daily schedule for full-time workers is less about finding more hours and more about placing the hours you already have where they matter most. If you have been lying awake wondering whether your job is unfair to your dog, take a breath: plenty of working owners raise calm, well-adjusted shepherds, and they do it with a plan, not with guilt.

Quick answer: Give your shepherd a real outing and a short training session before work, keep each stretch alone close to four hours (the figure UK vet charity PDSA gives as the longest a dog should routinely be alone), cover the middle of the day with a walker, a lunch break at home, daycare, or a trusted neighbor, and put the long walk, play, and training in the evening. Puppies need much shorter gaps. The planner below builds a timed schedule from your own work hours.

How long can a German Shepherd be left alone while you work?

For an adult shepherd, plan on stretches of about four hours, with every dog a little different. A typical eight-hour day plus commute is roughly double that, which is why most successful working owners split the day with at least one midday visit.

The PDSA's guidance on preventing separation anxiety puts it plainly: four hours is the longest that dogs should routinely be left alone, but every dog will be different. It adds that four hours can be too long for a very active young puppy and perfectly fine for an older dog who is used to it. "Routinely" is the key word. An occasional long day is not a disaster; a long day every day, with nothing breaking it up, is what wears a shepherd down.

Puppies run on a much shorter clock

Young puppies cannot simply wait out a workday. The Ohio State University Indoor Pet Initiative's crate-training guide uses a guideline of one hour for each month of age, give or take an hour, so a three-month-old might manage three to four hours. The same guide says never to confine a puppy longer than it can realistically be expected to hold it, and suggests an exercise pen, a baby-gated area, or a dog walker or neighbor when the workday is longer than that.

Why the breed makes this matter more

German Shepherds were bred to work beside a person all day. Many of them attach closely to one human, which is the same trait we explain in why your German Shepherd follows you everywhere. That loyalty is a gift, but it means alone time is a skill you teach on purpose, not something the dog figures out by itself.

What does a good 9-to-5 routine for an adult German Shepherd look like?

A good adult routine has three anchors: a purposeful morning session, a midday break, and a generous evening block. If your dog is relaxed when you get home and settles after the evening walk, the routine is working.

Here is a sample for an owner who leaves at 8:00 and returns at 5:30, a common shape for a 9-to-5 job with a commute.

A real owner's version of this day

In an August 2026 thread titled "Realistic puppy routine for a 4 months old GSD?", one owner who works full time away from home with a six-year-old and a seven-month-old described almost exactly this shape. They wake at 5:00–5:30, give a 40-minute to one-hour sniff walk around 6:00, add about 15 minutes of training, fetch, or flirt pole for the youngster, and crate the pup at 7:00. A walker comes around 11:00, the owner works close enough to come home at 2:00, they return at 6:00 for outside play and another long walk, feed dinner around 8:00, and go to bed around 10:00. On the two days a week they work later, the pup goes to daycare. Their bottom line: no fewer than two walks a day, morning and late afternoon, and constant, consistent training mixed into everything.

How do you schedule a German Shepherd puppy around a full-time job?

You schedule a puppy in short blocks: frequent potty breaks, several mini training sessions, and someone stopping by every few hours. For the first months, that usually means two midday visits, a work-from-home stretch, or puppy daycare.

Using the Ohio State guideline, a four-month-old has roughly a three-to-five-hour window, so a single lunch visit rarely covers a full workday. A practical puppy day looks like this:

  • Before work: potty, breakfast, a 10–20 minute walk or garden play, two or three 3–5 minute training sessions, then a nap in the pen.
  • Mid-morning visit (about 10:30): potty, short play, back to the pen with a chew.
  • Early-afternoon visit (about 14:00): potty, short walk, water, short training game.
  • Evening: potty on arrival, play, short walk, dinner, calm time, a late potty break before bed.

Owners in the same thread warned against long walks at this age. One suggested a rule of thumb they had heard, around five minutes per month of age twice a day, while another noted their four-month-old tired after about 20 minutes. Treat any such rule as a ceiling, follow your puppy's cues, and ask your vet what suits your dog. Puppies also spend energy mouthing, so if evenings turn into a nipping session, our guide to the German Shepherd puppy biting phase covers that side of the day.

Crate, pen, or room?

Short crate naps are fine, but a crate for the whole workday is a different thing. Wikipedia's crate training article cites behaviorist Steven Lindsay criticizing routine crating at night and again during the day for periods totaling 16 to 18 hours, a practice he says should not be condoned. Owners on r/germanshepherds landed in the same place: when one person described crating a nine-month-old from 7:30 to 5:00 with a single 30-minute sitter visit, a reply pointed out that the total came to almost 17 hours a day. The owner switched to a 45-inch playpen covering the living room, reported that the dog mostly slept and played with toys on camera, and said he was noticeably calmer and more fulfilled in the evenings.

Can you keep a German Shepherd if you work 10–12 hour shifts?

Yes, but only with daytime cover built in. A 12-hour shift plus commute is three or more four-hour stretches, so you need two visits, daycare, a household partner on a different shift, or a job where the dog can come along.

Shift workers often have one advantage: whole days off. Use them for the long hikes, new places, and longer training sessions that do not fit around a shift, and keep shift days simple and predictable.

Couples on rotating shifts have it easiest. One owner in a June 2026 thread on working full time said they and their spouse work opposite shifts, so their five-year-old is rarely home alone; they built up gradually from short absences to four to five hours, which they treat as the maximum without a check-in.

When the hours are simply too long

In an April 2026 r/germanshepherds post titled "I work 2 jobs, 60 hours a week," a young owner described a reactive male who was not coping. The replies were kind and practical: book a midday walker even two days a week, try a daycare that handles leash-reactive dogs, make the dog work for at least half his kibble with towel rolls and puzzle boxes that a family member can hand out, and if a better living situation is far off, consider cutting back to 40 hours. A few readers also raised rehoming through a rescue as a loving option. If you recognize yourself in that post, the honest step is to add cover, not more guilt.

How do you choose midday cover for your shepherd?

Choose the option that fits your dog's temperament first and your budget second. A sociable dog may love daycare, while a reactive or older shepherd often does better with a familiar walker or neighbor.

How do you teach a German Shepherd to be calm when home alone?

Teach it in small, boring steps, long before the first real workday. Reward calm, add time slowly, and keep your exits and returns low-key, so being alone feels normal rather than alarming.

The PDSA's method starts tiny: move a short distance away, return and reward calm; step out of the room for a moment, return and reward; then gradually extend the time, try short absences outside the house, and keep departures and arrivals calm. Safe puzzle or treat toys help fill the gap.

In this video, trainer Emily Larlham (Kikopup) walks through each stage of separation training on screen, from building a settle around food to adding duration, then distance, then real alone time, and shows the signs that you have moved too fast.

A useful detail from her approach: if your dog gets up to follow you or approaches the barrier, that is your signal to make the next step easier. You stay in control of the pace, and setbacks become rare instead of dramatic.

Adolescents need a refresher

A shepherd who coped well at four months can start testing limits at nine or twelve months. Keep alone-time practice going through adolescence, alongside the plan in our German Shepherd teenage phase monthly training plan.

Step-by-step: setting up your first working week

  1. Run the planner above with your real hours and note the longest stretch alone.
  2. Book cover for every stretch longer than about four hours for an adult, and far shorter for a young puppy.
  3. Prepare a safe area: a pen, gated room, or dog-proofed space with water, a bed, and a safe chew.
  4. Practice departures for a week or two before the job starts: keys, shoes, door, return in a minute, no fuss.
  5. Write the routine down for your walker or family so everyone handles the dog the same way.
  6. Use a camera if it calms you. Owners in the threads above who watch on camera mostly report the same thing: a dog that sleeps or plays quietly with toys.
  7. Review after two weeks. A dog who greets you calmly and settles in the evening is telling you the plan works. Destruction, constant barking, or frantic greetings mean you need more cover or more alone-time training.

Common mistakes working owners make

  • Only exercising the body. A long run builds a fitter dog; a 10-minute training session or food puzzle tires the brain too.
  • Dramatic goodbyes and hellos. Big emotional exits make departures feel like events. Keep them boring.
  • Skipping the midday break "just this once," every day. Routine is what counts, not the occasional exception.
  • Crating all day and all night. Short crate naps are fine; most of a day in a crate is not.
  • Jumping straight from 20 minutes alone to a full workday. Build the time up gradually instead.
  • Cramming the weekend. A huge Saturday hike does not make up for empty weekdays; steady daily structure matters more.
  • Letting guilt drive the plan. Feeling guilty is common, but a clear, written schedule helps your dog far more.

Frequently asked questions

Can I own a German Shepherd if I work 9 to 5?

Yes, if you build daytime cover into the plan. Give a real walk and training session before work, arrange a midday walker, neighbor, lunch break, or daycare so stretches alone stay near four hours, and keep evenings for the main exercise and training.

How many hours can an adult German Shepherd be home alone?

UK vet charity PDSA says four hours is the longest a dog should routinely be left alone, while noting every dog is different. An occasional longer day is manageable for a well-prepared adult, but a full workday alone every day is too much for most shepherds.

How long can a German Shepherd puppy be left alone?

Ohio State University's Indoor Pet Initiative uses one hour per month of age, give or take an hour, as a guideline for how long a puppy can be confined. A three-month-old might manage three to four hours, so most working owners need two midday visits for young puppies.

Is it better to crate or use a playpen while I'm at work?

A playpen or gated room usually suits long days better than a crate, because the dog can stretch, drink, and move between a bed and toys. Crates work well for naps and short absences once your dog is comfortable in one.

Should I walk my German Shepherd before or after work?

Both, if you can. A morning walk plus a short training session helps your dog rest while you are away, and the evening is usually the best time for the longest walk, play, and training of the day.

Is doggy daycare a good idea for a German Shepherd?

It can be, for sociable dogs, especially once or twice a week on long days. Reactive or easily overstimulated shepherds often do better with a familiar dog walker, so choose based on your dog's temperament.

The bottom line

A German Shepherd daily schedule for full-time workers works when the day has a shape your dog can predict: a real start, a break in the middle, and a rich evening. Keep stretches alone near four hours for adults, much shorter for puppies, teach alone time in small steps, and use the planner whenever your hours change. You do not need to be home all day; you need a plan you can repeat.
