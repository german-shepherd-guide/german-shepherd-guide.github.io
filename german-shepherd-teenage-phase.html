German Shepherd Teenage Phase Month by Month: 6–24 Months, Show vs. Working Line

A practical calendar with progress checks, without made-up developmental deadlines.

Draft prepared: October 14, 2026 · German Shepherd Guide · Human editorial review pending

Looking for a German Shepherd teenage phase month by month guide because yesterday's reliable puppy suddenly ignores you? This guide pairs a 6–24-month training calendar with observable progress checks, so you can adjust the work without pretending every shepherd follows the same schedule.

Quick answer: Adolescence can interrupt familiar training, but birthdays do not guarantee reliability. Use the calendar below as a suggested curriculum; advance when your dog can repeat a skill in the current setting, and keep practicing calm behavior alongside recall and leash work.

When does a German Shepherd become a teenager?

A training setback can fit adolescence, but no single starting or ending month applies to every German Shepherd. Separate a temporary change in responsiveness from a skill that was never practiced beyond the living room.

In the 2020 Newcastle-led research on teenage dogs, caregivers rated trainability lower at eight months than at five or twelve months in a questionnaire cohort of 285 dogs, including German Shepherds and other breeds or crosses. The separate obedience test involved 69 Labradors, Golden Retrievers, and their crosses, not a German Shepherd-only sample. These results support a temporary adolescent effect; they do not establish a month-by-month German Shepherd timetable or compare show and working lines.

Check the setting before blaming the age

Write down where the cue succeeds and where it fails. If your dog returns readily indoors but ignores you beside a playing dog, practice at a greater distance from that distraction. If returning only happens when food is already visible, work on rewarding after the response instead of displaying the reward beforehand.

Describe what you see rather than calling the dog stubborn: “looked toward another dog and did not turn” gives you a usable training problem. “Trying to dominate me” does not tell you which part of the exercise to change.

What should you work on from 6 to 24 months?

Use each month as a review point, not a forecast. The table is an editorial training curriculum: its tasks and progress checks are suggestions, not measured developmental milestones or promises of results.

Start with the row matching your dog's age, but move backward if the task is too difficult. Keep the same task for another month when necessary. A dog who learns quietly at home and struggles outside needs a change in practice conditions, not a longer checklist.

A practical way to read your month

If your twelve-month-old cannot pause during a toy game, use the nine-month practice suggestion. If your seven-month-old can check in outdoors, repeat it in another quiet place rather than racing ahead into busy crowds. This makes the calendar reusable instead of turning it into an exam your dog can fail.

Choose one check for your next outing. Observe before you prompt, practice the task, and note whether the response was easier, unchanged, or harder than last time. Keep a brief description of the surroundings beside that result.

Do show lines and working lines need different timelines?

The opened sources do not establish separate adolescent calendars for show-line and working-line German Shepherds. Use the line label to ask better questions about your dog's background; choose the training difficulty from the dog in front of you.

A working-line label is not evidence that every missed cue requires more exercise. A show-line label is not a guarantee that settling will happen without practice. Ask the breeder or previous handler what the parents enjoyed doing, how they behaved after activity, and what training the puppy already experienced.

Match the outlet to observed interest

Try an easy search for a familiar toy, a controlled exchange game, or a short sequence of known cues. Notice what keeps the dog engaged and what leaves them able to stop. Keep the activity that fits your household, rather than copying a sport routine because another shepherd has the same line label.

For a dog who shadows you around the house, our explanation of German Shepherd following behavior adds context to the difference between spending time together and practicing an independent resting routine.

How do you train through a setback without repeating commands?

Make the task easier, give one clear cue, and reward the response you want. When the dog cannot respond, change the setup rather than increasing your volume or repeating the word until it becomes background noise.

A step-by-step reset

  1. Define the response. For recall, decide whether success means reaching you, rather than merely looking in your direction.
  2. Pick an easy location. Begin in a quiet enclosed space where the dog is already able to pay attention.
  3. Prepare the reward. Keep it available without using it as a visible bribe before every cue.
  4. Ask once. If attention is elsewhere, pause and rebuild an easier opportunity rather than chanting the cue.
  5. Reward promptly. Make reaching you worthwhile, then release the dog back to an appropriate activity when possible.
  6. Change one feature. Add a little distance or a mild distraction; avoid making everything harder at once.
  7. Record the outcome. Note the location, distraction, response, and reward so the next session starts at a useful level.

For example, plan to practice returning in a fenced yard while another person sits quietly. If the dog cannot disengage from that person, increase the space or have the person leave. Do not add a moving toy at the same time. This is a suggested exercise setup, not a claim about a measured success rate.

Keep freedom separate from the calendar

Use an enclosed training area or supervised line management while practicing recall. Reaching eighteen or twenty-four months does not prove the dog will return around wildlife or other dogs. Permission to explore should follow what you have observed in comparable conditions, with appropriate containment maintained.

How can you teach the off switch alongside activity?

Give settling its own practice time in a quiet setting. Do not make the dog guess that a session has ended while you continue waving a toy or offering exciting interaction.

Dogs Trust's current settling guidance recommends a predictable routine, suitable physical activity, enrichment, rest, and practicing relaxation on a mat or bed. It also emphasizes training when the dog is calm and gradually practicing in new places.

Build a repeatable finish

Choose a quiet resting location. After a manageable activity, put the equipment away, reduce interaction, and reward calm behavior on the resting spot. Begin when the room is quiet; do not start with dinner guests, an open front door, and a noisy television competing for attention.

Observe the behavior you actually want: remaining on the mat with a loose posture, choosing to lie down, or staying settled while you take a seat. A motionless dog watching your treat hand is not the same observation as a dog relaxing. Keep your reward delivery quiet and adjust if it repeatedly gets the dog up again.

Separate mouthing practice from settling practice

If excited play turns into grabbing sleeves, prepare the end of the game before that moment. Keep an exchange toy ready and stop the interaction calmly when clothing becomes the target. Our guide to the German Shepherd puppy biting phase covers the earlier behavior context; here, focus on what happens immediately before and after adolescent play gets too intense.

A useful note might say, “Sleeve grabbing happened when the toy disappeared suddenly; the next session ended with an exchange.” That gives you a setup to compare, without inventing a universal month when mouthing must end.

What mistakes make teenage training harder to evaluate?

Changing too many variables and judging progress by one outing makes setbacks difficult to understand. Keep the exercise recognizable long enough to see which adjustment helps.

  • Turning every walk into a test. Include easy, rewarding practice instead of repeatedly asking for the hardest response.
  • Waiting for a birthday. Keep rehearsing the daily habits you want rather than expecting adulthood to teach them.
  • Escalating excitement to get engagement. Choose a quieter exercise if louder play prevents the dog from listening.
  • Ending every recall with lost access. Sometimes reward the return and allow appropriate exploration to continue.
  • Comparing different situations. A quiet driveway and a crowded trail are different training environments.
  • Using inconsistent household rules. Agree whether greeting requires paws on the floor and what happens when the dog succeeds.

When individual help makes sense

If you cannot manage the dog around everyday distractions, ask a qualified reward-based trainer to observe the routine. Bring your notes and a description of the environment. Persistent lunging, snapping, or guarding should not be dismissed as a phase you simply have to endure; arrange individual behavior support and prevent repeated confrontations while waiting.

Set a practical goal with the trainer, such as passing a distant dog with enough space to respond, rather than “be less teenage.” You should leave knowing which exercise to practice and when to make it easier.

How do you tell whether the plan is working?

Look for more repeatable responses in comparable settings, not an energetic dog becoming inactive. Measure the behavior you want to improve and preserve the conditions that made success possible.

Use a simple log: date, setting, cue, distraction, response, and what happened next. For a settling goal, describe whether your dog remained relaxed when you sat down. For leash walking, describe whether the dog checked in before reaching the end of the leash. Avoid mixing every behavior into a single “good dog” score.

The AKC's German Shepherd training guidance notes that adult maturity may come at two or three years and recommends continued work on obedience, impulse control, and focused activities across the nine-to-twenty-four-month period. That broader guidance is a reason to maintain training, not a countdown to guaranteed calm.

Frequently asked questions

Is eight months always the worst stage?

No. Eight months was an assessment point in the cited 2020 study, not a universal peak for every German Shepherd. Review your dog's behavior in its actual setting before assuming the calendar explains it.

Will my German Shepherd calm down at two years?

There is no guaranteed birthday for calm behavior. Keep practicing settling and daily skills; assess progress by repeatable responses rather than expecting the second birthday to replace training.

Do working lines stay teenage longer than show lines?

The sources used here do not establish different adolescent durations for the two lines. Your dog's background can inform your questions, but observed behavior should determine the practice plan.

Should I stop rewarding cues my dog already knows?

Continue making difficult responses worthwhile. If a cue works at home but fails around distractions, simplify the setup and reward successful practice instead of assuming the skill is complete everywhere.

How much exercise should a teenage shepherd get?

This guide does not prescribe a universal daily duration. Plan appropriate activity, mental engagement, and rest around the individual dog; increasing exercise alone is not a substitute for teaching recall, leash skills, and settling.

What if my dog is ahead of or behind the monthly table?

Use the row that matches the current training need. The calendar is a suggested curriculum, not a developmental test. Repeat easier tasks or transfer a mastered skill to a new quiet setting as appropriate.

A useful German Shepherd teenage phase month by month plan gives you a practice task and a way to check it. Choose one daily difficulty, simplify its setting, reward the response you want, and record what changes. Keep the line label as background information and let repeatable behavior determine your next step.
