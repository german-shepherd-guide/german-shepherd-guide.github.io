Working From Home With a German Shepherd: How to Teach a Settle on a Mat Under the Desk and Break Up the Shadow Habit

Working from home with a German Shepherd often means a warm dog on your feet and a nose at your elbow during every call. Our short verdict: keep your office buddy, but give the dog one clear spot. A mat at the desk, taught in small steps, is the first thing we would try for a calm dog that just likes to stay close.

In the video below, UK behaviourist Rosie Bescoby teaches a dog to settle in a bed while the owner works from home. Watch where the rewards land and how calm the person stays. It was published in January 2021.

Quick answer: Put a mat just outside the path of your chair. Pay your dog for stepping on it, then for lying down, then for staying a little longer. Add your own movements one at a time. Use a release word. Plan meetings around what your dog can do today, not what you hope for next month.

Why does working from home with a German Shepherd turn into a shadow habit?

You sit still for hours. You are easy to find. Then you get up many times a day: coffee, the door, the printer, the bathroom. A dog that likes to stay close learns that each chair squeak might mean something interesting.

The nickname for this is "velcro dog." In her essay on life with a velcro dog, veterinarian Dr. Karyn Kanowski describes Kodah, a Shepherd and Malinois cross. In her office he settles under the desk (good) or behind the wheels of her chair (not so good). That split is the whole point of this guide. The goal is not a dog in another room. The goal is a dog in the good spot.

These are self-picked forum stories, not a survey. They still sound familiar.

Plenty of owners love the company. You only need to change things if the dog blocks your chair, breaks into calls, or cannot rest while you move around. For the wider reasons behind following, read why German Shepherds follow their owners everywhere.

What do you need for a settle on a mat?

Very little. You can start with things you already own.

  • A mat. The RSPCA New South Wales "chill" handout suggests a special mat that is not the usual bed, something portable such as a bath mat, used only for this training. The same handout says never to use it as punishment.
  • Small, soft rewards. Pick food your dog likes but can eat quietly. Count it as part of the day's food.
  • A release word. "OK" or "Free" tells the dog the job is over.
  • One long-lasting chew or stuffed toy that your dog already handles well, saved for calls.
  • A timer. Your phone is fine.

You do not need to buy a special product. If you do shop, look for a mat that does not slide on your floor and is big enough for a grown Shepherd to lie flat.

Where should the mat go: under the desk or beside it?

Under the desk is cozy, but it is not always the best first spot. A big dog needs room to stand up and turn around. Your chair needs room to roll. Check both before you teach anything, because moving the mat later means practising again.

Tidy cables out of reach first. If your window faces the street, turn the mat away from it. A dog that watches the sidewalk is on duty, not resting. Our alert barking plan covers the doorbell moments that can break a settle.

How do you teach a German Shepherd to settle on a mat under the desk?

Start on a quiet day, not five minutes before a meeting. Keep sessions short. Move to the next step when the current one feels easy.

Step 1: Make the mat worth a visit

Lay it down and let your dog sniff it. Battersea's settle guide suggests throwing treats onto the mat instead of feeding from your hand, so the mat itself becomes the good thing.

Step 2: Let your dog work it out

Wait for a paw on the mat, mark it with a click or a soft "Yes," and toss the treat just off the mat so the dog can come back. Karen Pryor Academy's shaping method builds from one paw to four, then waits quietly for the dog to offer a sit and later a down. Stay silent and let the dog think.

Step 3: Name it, then add a release

Once your dog heads to the mat readily, say "Mat" just before they go. Reward on the mat. Then say your release word and let them leave. The household command poster helps everyone at home use the same words.

Step 4: Build time in tiny pieces

The RSPCA handout starts by waiting 2 seconds before the reward, then 5 seconds, and carries on from there. Mix in some short, easy waits so the dog does not learn that it only gets harder.

Step 5: Add your desk movements, one at a time

This is the part that breaks up the shadow habit. Sit down in your work chair. Reward calm. Then practise each move on its own:

  1. Type for a few seconds.
  2. Swivel or roll the chair a little, away from the dog.
  3. Half stand, then sit again.
  4. Stand fully, then sit.
  5. Take one step, come back, reward on the mat.
  6. Walk to the office door and return.
  7. Step out of sight for a moment and come back before your dog gets up.

Battersea also moves away one step at a time once the dog is comfortable. If your dog follows, go back to the last easy move. No scolding. Just make the next try simpler.

Step 6: Rehearse a fake call

Talk to an empty screen. Laugh. Say goodbye. Your dog may react to your call voice or your "goodbye" tone, so practise them while nothing is at stake.

What does a real workday with the mat look like?

Do not ask for a settle all day. Your dog needs time to walk, sniff, play, drink and nap wherever they like. Use the mat for blocks that matter, then release.

Is it easy to keep up?

The setup takes minutes. The habit takes longer. The hard part is remembering to reward the quiet dog while you are busy. Leave a closed treat jar on the desk and tie one reward to a work habit, such as each time you send an email. Young dogs may wobble for a while; the teenage phase planner can help you set a smaller goal.

The mat wins because you can take it to the kitchen, a friend's house or a café. A gate wins when the meeting is this afternoon and training started this morning. Use both. Never shut a dog into a crate or room that frightens them.

Pros, cons and our verdict

What we like

  • It gives the dog a job that is close to you.
  • It keeps the chair path clear.
  • It costs little or nothing.
  • Anyone in the house can use the same cue.

What can go wrong

  • Progress is uneven. A good Monday can be followed by a bouncy Tuesday.
  • It is easy to pay only when the dog gets up, which teaches getting up.
  • A large dog may not fit under a small desk.
  • It will not fix real distress about being apart from you.

Best for, and skip if

Best for: a relaxed but nosy German Shepherd, home workers with a few calls a day, and puppies or teens learning to switch off in short rounds.

Skip the desk drill for now if your dog panics, howls, drools heavily or damages doors when separated, or guards your office with growls or lunges. Get help from your vet or a qualified behaviourist first.

Our score: 8 out of 10 as a desk-day tool for calm followers. It loses points because it takes patience and cannot rescue a call on day one. Bottom line: teach the spot, pay the calm, and keep your shadow monster out from under the wheels.

Frequently asked questions

Can my German Shepherd just sleep under my desk?

Yes, if there is room to stand and turn, cables are out of reach and your chair cannot roll into a paw or tail. If it is cramped, put the mat beside the desk.

How long should the first mat sessions be?

Keep them short and stop while it still goes well. Start with a wait of a couple of seconds before the reward and build from there.

What if my dog gets up every time I stand?

Make standing smaller. Shift your weight, reward calm, then half stand. Move to a full stand only when the half stand is boring.

Should I use the dog's normal bed?

The RSPCA New South Wales handout suggests a separate mat used only for this training. That makes the signal clear and easy to carry.

What if my dog barks during a meeting?

Mute, move calmly away from the trigger and give an easy cue. Practise the doorbell or hallway sound later, outside work hours.

Does following me mean separation anxiety?

Not by itself. Many dogs just like company. If your dog is distressed when apart from you, ask your vet or a qualified behaviourist.

Do I have to stop all the following?

No. You only need your dog to be able to stay put when you ask, and to rest while you move. The rest of the day they can tag along if you both enjoy it.

Your first step today

Pick the spot, put the mat down and reward three visits before you open your laptop. That is enough for day one. Working from home with a German Shepherd gets easier when your dog knows where "close to you" is.
